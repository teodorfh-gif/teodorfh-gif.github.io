### Problem 1 (15%)

Brattli Arkitekter is an architecture office. Every project it handles needs five tasks: technical drawings, building-code checks, site reports, cost estimates and client meetings. One architect-hour produces one task-unit of any task and costs w = 480 kroner. The office can instead rent AI services. One AI-service unit costs r = 2400 kroner and produces Aᵢ task-units in task i:

| Task | AI productivity Aᵢ | Baseline cost share χᵢ |
|---|---|---|
| Technical drawings | 10 | 2/5 |
| Building-code checks | 8 | 1/5 |
| Site reports | 5 | 3/20 |
| Cost estimates | 5/2 | 3/20 |
| Client meetings | 3/5 | 1/10 |

The cost shares refer to the cost of a fixed project when architects do every task. Quality is the same with either method, and there are no setup costs. When the two methods cost the same, the task stays with labor.

(a) Write the cost of one task-unit with each method in general notation. Explain why architect output and AI output are perfect substitutes within a task, using a maximum of 2 sentences. Derive the cutoff for Aᵢ above which a task goes to AI, and find the set of automated tasks at w = 480 and r = 2400.

(b) Compute the saving πᵢ on each task, measured as a share of the task's labor cost, and the fixed-weight saving index G = Σᵢ χᵢπᵢ. A fixed project costs 320 000 kroner when architects do every task. How many kroner does the office save on that project, and what does it pay for the project?

(c) The architects' wage rises to w = 600, with r, Aᵢ and χᵢ unchanged. Find the new automated set, the new savings and G. Find the cost of the same project when architects do every task at the new wage, the saving in kroner and what the office now pays. Compare G and the office's payment before and after the wage rise. Explain why both move in the direction you found, using a maximum of 3 sentences.

(d) The office's country has a labor force E = 240 and makes design services (good x) and food (good y) with labor alone. One unit of food needs a_y = 1 hour. One unit of design services needs aₓ = 2 hours. AI adoption across the design sector lowers this to aₓ = 8/5, with a_y unchanged. Draw the production possibility frontier before and after the change, with design services on the horizontal axis. Give the intercepts and the slopes. Compute the factor by which labor productivity in design services rises. State when the relative price equals the opportunity cost, and explain what the shift in the frontier alone cannot tell us about the price of design services and about who loses. Use a maximum of 4 sentences.

### Problem 2 (12%)

Fonna and Tarva make two goods, electric ferries (x) and wool sweaters (y), with labor alone. Labor moves freely between the two industries in a country, but not between countries. The hours of labor needed per unit and the labor forces are:

| | Ferries | Sweaters | Labor force |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fonna | aₓ = 2 | a_y = 3 | E = 36 |
| Tarva | aₓ* = 6 | a_y* = 4 | E* = 108 |

Consumers in both countries have the utility function u = x^(2/5) y^(3/5). Sweaters are the numeraire, p_y = 1, and p = pₓ/p_y is the relative price of ferries.

(a) Use the condition that a country making both goods pays the same wage in both industries to derive the autarky relative price in general notation. Find its value in each country. Who has the absolute advantage and who has the comparative advantage in each good?

(b) The countries open to free trade. State which good each country produces. Derive the world price p in general notation from market clearing for ferries, then find its value. Find the wage in each country, measured in sweaters, and show with unit costs that each country is the cheaper producer of exactly one good. Explain what sets the pattern of trade and what sets the level of each wage, using a maximum of 2 sentences.

(c) Draw Fonna's production possibility frontier with ferries on the horizontal axis. Mark the specialization point B, draw the trade line through B and mark Fonna's consumption point C. Compute the coordinates of C and show that C lies above the frontier.

(d) Immigration raises Fonna's labor force to E = 84, with everything else unchanged. Draw the stair-step world relative supply and the relative demand, with the world output ratio of ferries to sweaters on the horizontal axis and p on the vertical axis. Show that complete specialization by both countries is no longer an equilibrium. Find p and each country's output of each good. State which country gains from trade compared with autarky at the new labor force, and explain why, using a maximum of 3 sentences.

### Problem 3 (8%)

Sundland is a small economy that makes hand-built boats (good x) and aluminium (good y) with labor E and capital K. It has E = 120 units of labor and K = 80 units of capital, and it produces both goods. Both industries are competitive, so price equals unit cost and profits are zero. One unit of boats needs 3 units of labor and 1 unit of capital. One unit of aluminium needs 1 unit of labor and 2 units of capital. Sundland takes world prices as given, and these are pₓ = 45 and p_y = 40. Write w for the wage and r for the rental rate of capital.

(a) Rank the two industries by factor intensity, showing the ratio you compare. Write the zero-profit conditions in general notation, and find w and r.

(b) Write the full-employment conditions and find the outputs x and y. The labor force then grows to E = 150, with K and goods prices unchanged. Find the new outputs. Compute the percentage changes in x, y and E, arrange them in one chain and name the theorem. State what happens to w and r, and explain why, using a maximum of 2 sentences.

(c) Return to E = 120. The world price of aluminium rises to p_y = 50, while pₓ stays at 45. Find the new w and r. Compute the four percentage changes, arrange them in one chain and name the theorem. Do workers gain or lose in real terms? Do capital owners? Explain why you need no information about what people consume to answer. Maximum 3 sentences.

### Problem 4 (5%)

Lindland and Ostmark are similar rich countries. In each of them one differentiated industry, kayaks, operates under monopolistic competition: many firms, each with its own variety, a fixed cost and a constant marginal cost, free entry and exit, and consumers who love variety. No calculation is needed in this problem. Answer in words, within the sentence limits.

(a) (2 points) A kayak firm charges a price above its marginal cost, yet free entry drives its profit to zero. Explain how both can hold. Maximum 3 sentences.

(b) (2 points) Lindland and Ostmark open to trade with each other. The elasticity of demand is not constant, as in the general version of the model. Explain what happens to the markup, to the real wage, to the number of firms in each country and to the number of varieties a consumer can buy. Name the evidence from Canada that this result connects to. Maximum 4 sentences.

(c) (1 point) Trace the chain of reasoning from increasing returns to two-way trade between identical countries. Maximum 2 sentences.

### Problem 5 (10%)

Ten multiple-choice questions on key concepts, one point each. Exactly one answer is correct. Write the question number and the letter.

**1.** The isoquant between AI and labor is L-shaped. The elasticity of substitution between AI and labor is
- (a) infinite
- (b) equal to one
- (c) zero
- (d) equal to w/r

**2.** An industry makes its output with AI and labor alone under constant returns. It is competitive, so profits are zero, and the output price is fixed. The rental rate of AI falls and the elasticity of substitution is above one. The wage
- (a) rises, since unit cost must still equal the fixed output price
- (b) falls, since labor's cost share falls
- (c) stays the same, since only the price of AI changed
- (d) cannot be signed without the firm's output

**3.** The rental rate of AI falls step by step, and at one point a task reaches its cutoff and moves to AI. At that point the fixed-weight saving index G
- (a) jumps up by the task's full cost share
- (b) stays continuous, while its slope changes
- (c) jumps down, since a new task carries a low saving
- (d) stops changing for all lower rental rates

**4.** Countries in the two-good Ricardian model often specialize completely. This result
- (a) shows that the model is simply wrong
- (b) follows from low foreign wages
- (c) is needed for any gain from trade
- (d) is an artifact of the two-good grid and disappears in the continuum version

**5.** Feenstra's empirical case for the Ricardian model rests on
- (a) a direct test that confirms every prediction of the model
- (b) the equality of wages across countries
- (c) the failure of the Heckscher-Ohlin-Vanek sign and rank tests
- (d) the absence of any productivity differences across countries

**6.** In the specific-factors model, labor moves between two sectors and capital is fixed in each one. The price of good x rises by 10 percent and the price of good y does not change. The wage
- (a) rises by more than 10 percent, so the real wage rises for every worker
- (b) rises by exactly 10 percent, so the real wage does not change for any worker
- (c) does not change, since labor moves freely
- (d) rises by less than 10 percent, so the real wage effect depends on the worker's consumption bundle

**7.** Trefler estimated productivity parameters for countries in the Heckscher-Ohlin-Vanek model. Their correlation with wages is about 0.9. This is evidence that
- (a) the fitted parameters are economically meaningful
- (b) technologies are identical across countries
- (c) the model passes its tests with identical technologies
- (d) the Leontief paradox is an unresolved refutation of Heckscher-Ohlin

**8.** A typical estimate of the gravity equation gives
- (a) GDP coefficients near zero and a positive distance elasticity
- (b) GDP coefficients near one and a distance elasticity of roughly −1 to −1.4
- (c) GDP coefficients near two and a distance elasticity near zero
- (d) GDP coefficients near one and a distance elasticity above one

**9.** Multilateral resistance in a gravity equation is best described as
- (a) a country's relative remoteness from all its trading partners
- (b) the physical distance between one pair of countries
- (c) the sum of the two countries' GDP
- (d) the markup a firm charges abroad

**10.** In the monopolistic competition model, firms sell differentiated varieties because
- (a) consumers dislike variety at home
- (b) wages differ across the two countries
- (c) increasing returns force market power, and market power requires a variety of one's own
- (d) each country has a different technology
